- Promoted the glucose post as the feed’s first glucose-percentile claim of the audited record: “My blood glucose is lower than 99.8% of humans / best month I’ve had: / > mean 79 mg/dL / > best week 77 mg/dL / > HbA1c 4.6% / Elevated blood glucose rusts from within.” (424 likes, 12 reposts, 80 replies at re-read; November 10, 19:16 UTC, with a photo). Verified: the internal number-pair is consistent under the standard translation — Nathan et al., ADAG study, Diabetes Care 2008 (507 subjects; eAG = 28.7 × A1C − 46.7) puts HbA1c 4.6% at eAG 85.3 mg/dL, within the regression’s ±15.7 mg/dL prediction error of the claimed 79 mg/dL CGM month mean (which back-translates to ~4.4% A1c) — but the translation at this end is an extrapolation: Labcorp’s reporting guidance calculates eAG only for HbA1c 5.0–15.0% because “estimations in the nondiabetic range have not been verified,” and a CGM interstitial average is not venous plasma. Verified: “rusts from within” works as glycation metaphor — elevated glucose accelerates nonenzymatic glycation and advanced glycation end-product (AGE) formation, and the AGE–RAGE axis drives the oxidative-stress and inflammatory cascades of diabetic complications (Nature Reviews Endocrinology 2008) — pathology demonstrated at diabetic-range glucose, not a linear dose–response into the low-normal range. Verified in direction, with the stop the post doesn’t mention: EPIC-Norfolk found HbA1c continuously associated with cardiovascular disease and mortality across the whole nondiabetic distribution, lowest rates below 5% (Khaw et al., BMJ 2001; Ann Intern Med 2004), but the cohort’s own 11.2-year reanalysis of 17,196 nondiabetic adults (Diabetologia 2011, 10.1007/s00125-011-2162-0) found all-cause-mortality hazard “virtually constant in the low range,” rising only above ~5.5% — a “basement” below which no mortality benefit of further lowering was demonstrated, and 4.6% sits 0.9 percentage points below it. Boundaries recorded: “99.8% of humans” is against an unnamed cohort that includes the world’s diabetic and prediabetic population, not healthy peers — the same unpublished-percentile pattern as the Prenuvo and Kernel readouts; the three numbers are summary figures with no device, dataset, or method published; and clinically low HbA1c can also reflect shortened red-cell lifespan (hemolysis, blood loss, anemia) rather than superior metabolic health. The dashboard records an attributed N=1 metabolic readout with verified eAG arithmetic and epidemiologic context — not evidence that pushing HbA1c below 5% extends life, a reader glucose target, or medical advice.
