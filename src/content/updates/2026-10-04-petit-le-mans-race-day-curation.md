# Petit Le Mans race-day curation

- Reviewed everything after the October 3 publication (last covered post: October 2, 20:26 UTC). No newer durable batch existed under `knowledge/raw/x/` (the wiki itself has been quiet since September 5), so this pass ran a bounded read-only Bird timeline audit (`bird user-tweets @bryan_johnson -n 50 --json`, 50 posts spanning September 11–October 3). Result: three posts newer than the last covered post, all from October 3 race day at Motul Petit Le Mans.
- Promoted the race-day arc as one algorithmic-health update. The pre-race post (15:10 UTC) laid out the physiological case for Lütke’s 2.5-hour stint—over 180 mph, up to 4.2 g (“715 lbs of force” on the body, “67 lbs” on the head), braking at “up to 200 lbs of leg force,” a 104–122°F cockpit, claimed 102°F core temperature, 140–170 bpm heart rate, and up to 5 kg of sweat in an LMP2 with no driver cooling, in the rain—and the post-stint post (22:02 UTC) closed the arc the October 3 publication had recorded as “announced, not published”: “We completed a first-in-world racing experiment. I collected Tobi’s blood before and after his 2.5 hr race. This blood test is unique. It’s deep molecular profiling.” Checked the internal arithmetic (4.2 g × ~170 lb ≈ 714 lb; 4.2 g × ~16 lb ≈ 67 lb; 5 kg ≈ 11 lb — all self-consistent) and kept the boundaries visible: the headline numbers are Johnson’s own with no per-race telemetry source, the 5 kg/2.5 h figure implies a ~2 L/h sweat rate (roughly triple the ~0.6 L/h of measured driver studies, per Carlson et al., J Strength Cond Res, 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002268), and “deep molecular profiling” names no panel, analyzer, or analysis plan.
- Promoted the “car racing is cognitive enhancement” post (15:22 UTC) as a second signal: three research claims that compress two real literatures. Verified the first and third against Anguera et al. (Nature, 2013, 10.1038/nature12486): 12 hours of adaptive multitasking training on the NeuroRacer 3-D driving game left 60–85-year-olds with multitasking costs better than untrained 20-year-olds (−16.2% vs −36.7% cost), gains persisting six months without booster sessions. Verified the second (“race car drivers do in one brain region what a novice does in 15”) as a compression of neural-efficiency findings: Bernardi et al. (PLoS ONE, 2013, 10.1371/journal.pone.0077764) found professional racing drivers recruit a smaller volume of task-related regions than naïve drivers, and the structural follow-up (Frontiers Hum Neurosci, 2014, 10.3389/fnhum.2014.00888) found greater motor-control/spatial-navigation recruitment with retrosplenial gray-matter density correlating with racing success. The boundary kept visible: no cited study shows actual race-car driving enhancing cognition in drivers—the evidence is a custom video game in older adults and cross-sectional expert/novice comparisons that cannot separate training from selection, and “one brain region vs 15” is a compression, not a measured count.
