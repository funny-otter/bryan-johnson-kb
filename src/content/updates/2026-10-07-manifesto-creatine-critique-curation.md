- Promoted the water-retention critique of the viral creatine trial and verified its checkable claims. The trial is real: Chun, Kreider et al., *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 2026 (10.1080/15502783.2026.2716273; ISRCTN83081058) — 73 sedentary adults aged 45–65 enrolled, 64 completers, participants chose their exercise arm with only the supplement randomized double-blind, 10 g/day creatine monohydrate versus maltodextrin placebo for 12 weeks, DXA body composition. The quoted thread's 2.43-lb versus 0.31-lb pair matches the paper's no-exercise-creatine +1.1 kg versus exercise-placebo +0.14 kg lean-tissue changes. Johnson's two design points verify: exercise was not randomized, and DXA cannot separate muscle protein from water — a limitation the trial itself could not rule out because it reports no body-water measurement. The boundary kept visible in both directions: "It was water retention" is a mechanism asserted as a finding (the same no-exercise creatine arm also increased 1RM strength, which intracellular hydration alone does not obviously explain), the no-exercise arms were small (about 13 completers each; lean-mass CI 0.2–2.0 kg), the trial's larger body-fat-percentage reduction belonged to the exercise-plus-diet arm where real fat loss was the design, and the senior author's creatine-industry ties (AlzChem supplied the creatine) are disclosed rather than hidden.
