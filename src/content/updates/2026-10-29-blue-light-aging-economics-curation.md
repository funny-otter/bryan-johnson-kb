- Promoted the aging-economics relay with the paper verified: “Slowing aging after the age 50 can add 3.6 yrs of life and is worth $4 million in economic value” (437 likes, 15 reposts, 56 replies), with an attached screenshot OCR-identified as Felizzi, “Economic impact of biomarker-based aging interventions on healthcare costs and individual value,” Frontiers in Genetics, published October 11, 2026 (10.3389/fgene.2026.1814599). Verified: a single-author Hypothesis & Theory modeling paper by an enGene (Basel) industry employee, chaining the DunedinPACE epigenetic clock (via Mak et al. 2023 frailty coupling, 10.1093/gerona/glad251) to Swiss-calibrated mortality, frailty-prevalence, and healthcare-cost data across three intervention scenarios from age 50. The lifespan figure verifies as a round-up: the most optimistic scenario (CR2, interventions holding the pace of aging at 1.0) yields a 3.58-year life-expectancy gain — the moderate CR1 scenario gives 2.89. The money figure drifts twice: the paper’s most optimistic total individual value is CHF 3,236,195 (≈$3.5–3.6M at recent CHF/USD rates, so “$4 million” overstates the paper’s own figure by roughly a tenth), and it is a willingness-to-pay construct — CHF 1,216,186 survival value plus CHF 2,020,009 health-quality value, undiscounted, in the Murphy–Topel/Scott value-of-life framework — not cash, income, or savings: the modeled healthcare-system savings are CHF 131,608 per person over 40 years, and the healthcare case turns cost-sensitive once CHF 600 twice-yearly DunedinPACE testing is included. Boundary recorded: CR2 assumes an intervention can hold biological aging pace at 1.0 — assumed, not demonstrated by any real intervention; DunedinPACE captures one DNA-methylation axis of aging by the paper’s own caveat; costs and life tables are Swiss; and the author’s framing explicitly supports consumer longevity-medicine business models. The post names no study.
- Promoted the stage-lights blue-light anecdote as the feed’s second evidence-aligned sleep post in two days: “The lights pointing at the stage were blasting my eyeballs with blue light which makes it harder to fall asleep and fragments sleep. / So we hung out in the dark and it was so much better.” (761 likes, 7 reposts, 73 replies). Verified direction: in a 5-night randomized crossover, reading a light-emitting e-book vs a print book suppressed evening melatonin by 55.12%, delayed dim-light melatonin onset more than 1.5 hours, lengthened sleep latency by ~10 minutes (25.65 vs 15.75 min), reduced REM sleep, and left readers sleepier the next morning (Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy, Czeisler, PNAS 2015, 10.1073/pnas.1418490112), with the device spectrum peaking at 452 nm in the blue range. Boundary recorded: the lab exposed participants at controlled illuminance for fixed durations while the post is an unmeasured N=1 event anecdote (no lux, duration, or sleep data; “so much better” is subjective), the post names no study, and evening-light avoidance is general sleep hygiene, which the AASM suggests against as a stand-alone therapy for chronic insomnia disorder.
