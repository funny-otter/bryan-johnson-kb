# Kernel

Kernel is [bryan johnson](/knowledge/bryan-johnson/)'s neurotechnology company focused on measuring brain activity / building non-invasive brain-interface technology. Johnson's own site frames Kernel as the next measurement frontier after DNA/genome cost collapse: “What we measure, we improve.”

## Thesis

Kernel's thesis is that the brain and mind are under-measured relative to other biological systems. The Chicago Booth profile quotes Johnson arguing that if humans could really measure brain activity, it could be a historical turning point for cognitive evolution. It connects the company to Johnson's depression history, family experiences with addiction/Alzheimer's, and a broader ambition to improve cognition and harmony between people.

## Relationship to Blueprint

Kernel and [blueprint protocol](/knowledge/blueprint-protocol/) share the same deep pattern: measure a system that is currently opaque, use the measurements to guide intervention, and build technology/community around improved feedback loops. Johnson's 2020 “I fired myself” essay explicitly gestures toward the absence of an “Activity Monitor” for the brain and adds: “As Kernel CEO... Yet. We don't have the numbers. Yet.”

## The August 2026 percentile post

Johnson’s brain-measurement thesis became a public self-interpretation exercise on August 30, 2026, when he posted regional percentiles from his own Kernel measurements—amygdala 95th (emotion/threat detection), putamen 99th (learning/habit formation), caudate 97th (goals/decisions/working memory), globus pallidus 93rd (movement control), and frontal gray matter at the 78th percentile, reported up 1.35% over two years “when it should be going down with age”—and mapped each region onto his own personality: processing hate as information rather than threat, near-effortless habit formation, juggling founder, content-creator, and “rejuvenation athlete” threads. He cited twin-study heritability of roughly 0.75–0.89 for the subcortical regions and gestured at real plasticity literature without naming it: adults who learned to juggle grew measurable gray matter (Draganski et al., 2004, *Nature*; a transient, selective expansion in visual-motion cortex hMT/V5 and the left posterior intraparietal sulcus over roughly three months, which receded once practice stopped), and a randomized trial in which six months of aerobic exercise grew frontal gray and white matter in sedentary adults aged 60–79 (Colcombe et al., 2006, *J Gerontol A*). The post names no study, the normative cohort behind the percentiles is undisclosed, and Johnson himself closes with “speculative for my n of 1 context.” The dashboard records an attributed self-report with literature context—not a validated brain-health metric, evidence that his protocol grew his frontal cortex, or medical advice.
